#### Most cursors follow.

#### Mine reacts.

A dot for precision. A ring for presence. Two shapes, one job: make the page feel touched, not just clicked.

## Start with everything. Ship with less

The first version did five things. Pointer states. Text labels. Magnetic pull. Blend modes. Takeover shapes.

Five features, zero identity.

**More states impress the builder. Fewer states impress the user.**

So I cut three. Kept two:

**Invert** — the ring flips whatever color sits beneath it. Works on light. Works on dark. Doesn't ask which.

**Takeover** — the ring stops following and becomes the button. Same radius. Same shape. Same edges.

Everything else got deleted.

## Why invert, not tint

A tinted cursor makes assumptions. Light background, dark ring. Dark background, light ring. Guess wrong, cursor disappears.

Invert doesn't guess.

It reads the pixel underneath and flips it — automatically, everywhere, forever.

**One rule beats ten conditionals.**

## Why takeover, not hover

A hover state decorates the button. A takeover _becomes_ it.

The ring doesn't scale up near your subscribe button. It disappears into it — same corners, same fill, same shape — so the click target and the cursor are, for a moment, the same object.

**Decoration fades. Identity sticks.**

## The part nobody sees

Underneath both states: one `mousemove` listener, two motion values, zero layout thrashing.

No `elementFromPoint` on every frame. No polling. The DOM tells you what's under the mouse — you just have to listen instead of asking.

**Performance is a feature. It's just invisible.**

## Why build a custom cursor at all

The OS gives you a cursor for free.

Free things don't remember you.

A cursor that inverts color and takes over shape does one thing a system arrow never will: it tells the visitor _this page was designed, not assembled._

That's the whole pitch.

Two states. No excess. Try it below.
